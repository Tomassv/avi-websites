On 1 October 2026, five of the biggest names in European air travel formed the Responsible Biometrics Travel Industry Coalition: ACI Europe, IATA, SITA, Amadeus and Idemia Public Security (which Amadeus is in the process of acquiring). Their goal is to shape the EU's Digital Omnibus, the package meant to simplify Europe's digital and data rules, so that it makes room for voluntary biometrics at airports and across the journey.

Here is a short summary of what they are asking for, and why it matters.

## What the coalition is asking for

The group has published a manifesto with a few clear demands:

- **Consent as a legal basis.** Passengers who agree to use biometrics should be able to do so, with consent accepted as a valid legal basis under GDPR.
- **Technology-neutral rules.** The law should set safeguards, not pick specific technologies.
- **Room to test.** Supervised pilots and regulatory sandboxes, so new solutions can be tried under oversight.
- **Choice stays with the passenger.** Using biometrics must be voluntary and reversible, and passengers who opt out must always have a non-biometric alternative.

The coalition's view is that recent readings of GDPR principles such as necessity, data minimisation and proportionality have become too restrictive for travel.

## Why it's a problem today

Each biometric project currently has to show, country by country, that no less intrusive option would work as well. With 27 member states, the same design can be judged differently in each one. Industry voices say this makes it hard for airports and airlines to invest, and that EU airports risk falling behind hubs outside the EU that already offer faster, document-free processing.

## Do passengers want it?

The evidence points both ways. The coalition cites IATA's 2025 Global Passenger Survey, where 74% of respondents said they would prefer biometrics to passports and boarding passes, as long as their data is secure. Phocuswright research, on the other hand, found that only around a quarter to a third of travellers in the UK, Germany and France mostly or completely trust biometric airport access. Travellers warm up when the benefits are concrete: faster border processing, easier cross-border travel and less risk of identity theft.

## The bigger picture: digital identity

This debate sits alongside the EU's digital identity plans. Every member state must offer citizens a European Digital Identity (EUDI) wallet by the end of 2026. Digital travel credentials, which are verified copies of passport data stored on the passenger's own phone, could let one trusted identity be reused across airlines, airports and systems, with less data stored permanently by the industry.

## What it means for airlines

The Digital Omnibus is still moving through the legislative process, so nothing is settled. But the direction is worth watching. The rules on how airlines and airports may collect, use and keep passenger data are being redrawn, and consent, transparency and the ability to show how data was handled will matter well beyond the boarding gate.

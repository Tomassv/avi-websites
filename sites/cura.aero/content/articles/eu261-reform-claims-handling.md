On 15 June 2026, the European Parliament and the Council of the EU reached a political agreement on revising Regulation (EC) No 261/2004, the rules behind air passenger compensation in Europe. After around ten years of debate, the result is a set of targeted changes, not a new regulation. Much of it puts existing court rulings and industry practice into law. Some of it adds new, more specific duties on how airlines handle claims.

The final legal text hasn't been published yet, so details may still move. Here is what we know so far, and what it means for the teams who process claims every day.

## What changes

- **Cabin bags.** A standard cabin bag, on top of a personal item, must be included in the advertised base fare. Cheaper fares for passengers travelling light are expected to remain possible.
- **Family seating.** A child under 14 must be seated next to an accompanying adult at no extra cost.
- **Telling passengers their rights.** Within 96 hours of a disruption, airlines must tell affected passengers what they are entitled to and how to claim.
- **Passengers with reduced mobility.** Stronger protections during disruption and for mobility equipment. The details are still to come.
- **No-shows.** Airlines can no longer refuse boarding on the return flight because a passenger missed the outbound one.
- **Extraordinary circumstances.** A standard list will set out which events release an airline from paying compensation. This has been the most disputed part of EU261 in court, and it isn't yet clear whether the list will be closed or able to change over time.
- **Claims handling.** Airlines will have to acknowledge and answer claims within set time limits.

## What stays the same

The rules cover the same flights as today: every departure from the EU, and flights into the EU operated by EU airlines. Compensation for delays of more than three hours doesn't change:

- Flights up to 1,500 km: €250
- Flights from 1,500 to 3,500 km: €400
- Flights over 3,500 km: €600

These fixed amounts can still be several times the price of the ticket.

## What it means for claims teams

Clearer rules won't mean fewer claims. The claims culture EU261 created, including the companies that file claims on passengers' behalf, isn't going away. What changes is where the arguments happen. Expect disputes to move towards whether an event fits the new list of extraordinary circumstances, and whether the airline met its procedural duties: informing passengers in time, and acknowledging and answering claims within the limits.

That second point matters most day to day. Once a missed deadline is a breach in its own right, a claims process that runs on shared inboxes, spreadsheets and manual lookups becomes a risk. For every claim, an airline will need to know when it arrived, what the passenger was told and when the airline replied, and be able to show it.

## When it applies

The new rules take effect 12 months after the final text is published in the Official Journal of the EU. That leaves roughly a year from publication to adapt fares, seating, passenger communication and claims processes. The clock hasn't started yet, but the direction is clear, and a year goes quickly.
